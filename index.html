Global Explorer of GHG-Forced Temperature Changes

This interactive visualization uses CMIP6 greenhouse-gas-only near-surface air temperature data. The change is calculated as the 2000–2014 modern average minus the 1850–1880 early industrial baseline. Hovering over the map links each local change to its latitude-level average.

Global temperature change map

Color bins show temperature change in 0.75 K intervals. Hover over a grid cell to compare that local value with the average change at the same latitude.

Linked latitude chart

The line shows average change by latitude. The moving dots compare the hovered location against the latitude average.

Write-up

Motivation

It is well known that greenhouse gases are the dominant driver of long-term human-caused warming, however, it is less known that their effect is not uniform. For example, high latitudes warm far faster than the tropics. CMIP6 greenhouse-gas-only simulations lets us observe this effect isolated from other drivers. We built this visualization to illustrate where is warming concentrated, and how does a specific location compare to its broader latitude band?

Data transformation

We used near-surface air temperature from the CMIP6 greenhouse-gas-only simulation. We calculated temperature change by subtracting the 1850–1880 baseline average from the 2000–2014 modern average. To calculate the 1850–1880 baseline, and the 2000-2014 modern average we agreggated the data by taking the mean across all months and years in each latitude and longitude location.

Visual encoding

The main map uses longitude and latitude as position because the data is spatial. Temperature change is encoded using binned color categories instead of a continuous gradient. We chose bins because they make the magnitude of warming easier to compare and avoid making tiny color differences seem more precise than they are. We used a diverging color scale where blue represents cooling or negative change, while stronger red represents larger positive warming change. We chose these colors becuase they fit with people's preconcived notions of what warming and cooling look like, and because they are colorblind-friendly.

Interaction design

The main interaction is hover-based linking between the map and the latitude chart. When the user hovers over a grid cell, the map shows the exact local change, while the side chart highlights the corresponding latitude. The chart also compares the hovered local value with the average change at that latitude. This makes the interaction useful because the reader can see whether a specific location is above or below the broader latitude-level pattern. A line chart emphasizes the relationship between neighboring latitude bands, making it immediately clear where warming accelerates. Additionally, the temperature change is much more consistent with latitude than longitude.

Alternatives considered

We considered using a time slider, but decided to focus on a modern-minus-baseline change map because it directly answers our question of how different regions have been effected by climate change and keeps the interaction focused. We also considered aggregating the temperature change by country, instead of specific latitide and longitude grid cells because it would simplify the map to the viewer. But, we decided against it because we think it would loose to much information in the process.

Development process

We split the work into data preparation, exploratory analysis, D3 implementation, interaction design, styling, and writing. The data preparation step involved using Python to isolate the greenhouse-gas-only simulation, calculate the 1850–1880 baseline, calculate the 2000–2014 modern average, and export a smaller CSV for D3. Nathan And Calvin focused on this part as well as EDA. Jack focused on the D3 step, which involved building the binned change map, the linked latitude chart, the tooltip, and the hover interaction. Then, the rest of us spent time revising the final chart together, ensuring it followed the proper visual encoding and interaction design principles. And then, Jake did the writeup. Overall, we spent about a total of 20 hours on this project, with the most time-consuming parts being preparing the climate data into a simple D3-ready format and making the linked interaction clear enough for a first-time user.
